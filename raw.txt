What Is El Niño and Why Does It Matter?
El Niño is a natural climate pattern characterised by the warming of surface waters in the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean. When it occurs, it disrupts the normal flow of the east-to-west trade winds, spreading warmth across the tropical Pacific and altering weather patterns across the entire globe. The effects are far-reaching: El Niño typically brings severe droughts to Australia and Southeast Asia, heavier rainfall and flooding to parts of the United States and East Africa, warmer winters in Canada and northern Europe, and intensified hurricane activity in the eastern Pacific.
NOAA's Warning: El Niño Likely to Form in 2026
The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) issued an advisory confirming that El Niño is likely to form during the summer months of 2026 and persist through the end of the year — and potentially longer. The agency assessed a 1-in-3 chance of it becoming a "strong" El Niño event by winter 2026. This represents a significant upward revision from earlier forecasts, which had suggested only a modest event might develop.
The timing is particularly concerning. The two most recent El Niño events — in 2014–16 and 2023–24 — both brought record heat around the world that fuelled further global temperature increases. The 2023–24 event contributed directly to 2024 becoming the hottest year in recorded history. A new strong El Niño layered on top of the background warming already baked into the climate system could push 2026 or 2027 to break that record again.
What Does This Mean for the Rest of 2026?
For the United States, an El Niño summer typically brings above-average temperatures across most of the country, more active hurricane seasons in the eastern Pacific, and drought conditions across parts of the northern Plains and Pacific Northwest. For Asia, which is already struggling with disrupted energy supplies due to the Iran war, El Niño could deepen drought conditions that threaten rice and wheat harvests. For Sub-Saharan Africa, it brings an increased risk of drought in some regions and flooding in others.
Climate scientists note that the combination of human-caused background warming and natural El Niño variability creates a multiplicative effect on extreme weather events. In plain terms: when El Niño arrives on top of a planet that is already 1.3°C warmer than pre-industrial times, the most extreme heat events, droughts, and rainfall events all become worse than they would be from either factor alone.
What Can You Do to Prepare?
Individuals and households can take practical steps to prepare for an El Niño year: stock emergency water supplies if you live in a drought-prone area, review your home insurance coverage for flood risk, avoid planting drought-sensitive crops during periods of El Niño uncertainty, and stay informed through official NOAA and local meteorological service updates throughout the year.

The Rate Cut That Will Not Come
At the start of 2026, the consensus on Wall Street was clear: the Federal Reserve would cut interest rates two to three times during the year. Inflation was running at 2.4% in January — approaching the Fed's 2% target. The job market was healthy. The economic trajectory was positive. Rate cuts were not a question of "if" but "when." Then February 28 arrived. Within three weeks of the US and Israel striking Iran, those rate cut expectations had been almost entirely cancelled, according to CME Group data tracking fed funds futures markets. Some participants were now pricing in a small but nonzero probability of a rate hike — something that would have seemed absurd before the war.
Why the Iran War Changes the Fed's Calculus Completely
The Federal Reserve faces an extremely uncomfortable situation known in economics as a "supply shock" — an external event that simultaneously pushes prices up (inflationary) and slows economic growth (recessionary). When inflation is caused by too much demand, the Fed's standard tool — raising interest rates to cool borrowing and spending — works well. When inflation is caused by a supply disruption like an oil shock, raising rates can still tame prices but does so by deepening the economic slowdown, potentially triggering a recession. Cutting rates, on the other hand, would provide economic relief but risk letting inflation run hotter.
Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller captured the dilemma publicly. Speaking on CNBC, Waller — who dissented from the majority at January's meeting by voting for a rate cut — said that the longer the war goes on and the higher oil prices remain, "the more inflation is a concern." Treasury yields tell the same story: the 10-year Treasury yield jumped from 3.97% before the war to 4.38% within three weeks, a significant move in the bond market that directly translates into higher mortgage rates and borrowing costs.
What This Means for Your Money
For homebuyers: mortgage rates, which had been gradually declining, are now rising again. For existing homeowners with fixed-rate mortgages, there is no immediate impact. For those with variable-rate products or upcoming refinancing needs, the trajectory is unfavourable. For savers: higher yields on Treasury bonds and money market accounts mean better returns on safe savings vehicles. For investors: higher rates reduce the attractiveness of equities, particularly growth stocks with valuations dependent on low discount rates.
The economy's trajectory in 2026 now depends heavily on one variable: how long the Iran conflict lasts. A resolution within the next month, combined with a reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, would likely allow oil prices to normalise and rate cut expectations to partially recover. A war that drags into summer at current intensity would, according to most independent forecasters, tip the US into technical recession by the fourth quarter of 2026.

The Numbers Behind the Conflict
News coverage of the Iran war is dominated by military movements, oil prices, and diplomatic manoeuvring. But behind all of it is a human story of extraordinary suffering. According to Al Jazeera's confirmed casualty tracker as of March 22, the war has killed at least 1,444 people in Iran — including at least 204 children. At least 18 people have been killed in Israel by Iranian missile and drone strikes. Thirteen US service members have died, along with six additional crew members killed when a US refuelling aircraft crashed in western Iraq. At least 21 people have been killed in Gulf states — Bahrain, Kuwait, Jordan, and others — as Iranian attacks spread across the region.
These numbers are preliminary and likely to be significantly undercounted. Iran's media environment is heavily restricted during the conflict, and access for independent journalists is extremely limited. The real toll on Iranian civilians — killed by direct strikes, by the disruption of medical supply chains, or by the collapse of basic infrastructure — may not be known for months or years.
A New Year Under Fire: Nowruz and Eid in Wartime
The timing of the conflict has added a particularly painful cultural dimension for Iranians. March 2026 saw the rare alignment of two major holidays — Eid al-Fitr, the feast marking the end of Ramadan, and Nowruz, the Persian New Year celebrated by Iranians worldwide as the most important holiday of the year. Millions of Iranians gathered at holy sites including the Imam Reza Shrine in Mashhad to mark these occasions. Air defences were activated over Tehran on the first day of Nowruz as explosions were heard across the capital. Haft-sin tables — the traditional new year arrangements of symbolic items — stood in homes across Iran as bombs fell outside.
UNESCO World Heritage Sites in the Crossfire
The war has also inflicted damage on some of humanity's most irreplaceable cultural heritage. On March 2, a strike on Arg Square in Tehran damaged the nearby Golestan Palace, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. On March 9, strikes on the city of Isfahan damaged Naqsh-e Jahan Square, Chehel Sotoun, Ali Qapu Palace, the Shah Mosque, and the Jameh Mosque — all globally significant examples of Persian Islamic architecture. The Azadi Sport Complex in Tehran was bombed on March 5. On March 8, the Falak-ol-Aflak fortress, marked with a blue shield emblem signifying its protected cultural heritage status, sustained damage.
UNESCO issued a statement urging the protection of Iran's heritage sites, noting that sites marked with the blue shield — a symbol meant to invoke the same protection as the Red Cross for medical facilities — were being struck nonetheless. The damage to these sites is not simply an aesthetic loss. These are among the great monuments of human civilisation, irreplaceable repositories of art, architecture, and history that have survived for centuries. Their destruction erases something that belongs not just to Iran but to all of humanity.
A Conflict With No Quick End in Sight
As the war enters its fourth week, there is no ceasefire in sight. Trump says the US is considering "winding down" operations, but simultaneously threatening to escalate against Iranian power plants. Israel continues striking Tehran. Iran continues launching missiles at Gulf states and commercial shipping. The Strait of Hormuz remains largely closed. And behind all the geopolitical manoeuvring, ordinary people — Iranian families sheltering from airstrikes, Gulf workers caught in retaliatory attacks, American service members deployed far from home — are paying the heaviest price.
The world's attention is stretched between the geopolitics, the oil prices, and the stock markets. But the human beings at the centre of this conflict deserve to be seen and counted. Their lives and suffering are not a footnote to the economic story. They are the story.
