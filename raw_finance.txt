Why Is the KOSPI Crashing So Hard?
South Korea's benchmark KOSPI is today trading at 5,479.97 — down a brutal 301.23 points, or 5.21% from its previous close of 5,781.20. The day's range of 5,479.97 to 5,580.15 shows the index hit the floor of its intraday range almost immediately at the open and has not recovered. This is not a routine dip. Context matters here: the KOSPI hit a record high above 6,200 points in February 2026, just days before the Iran war began on February 28. Since that peak, the index has collapsed over 11%, erasing months of gains driven by South Korea's semiconductor supercycle.
The reason South Korea is being hit harder than almost any other market in the world is structural. As Morningstar's Asia director of equity research Lorraine Tan noted, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix together constitute nearly 50% of the KOSPI by market capitalisation. When these two semiconductor giants fall — Samsung down roughly 10-12% since the war began, SK Hynix similarly — the index falls far more dramatically than a diversified index would. On March 3, when the KOSPI suffered its single worst day in history, plunging 12.1% to 5,093.54 and triggering an exchange circuit breaker, Samsung alone lost nearly 12% of its value in a single session after a report revealed that mass production at its US plant in Taylor, Texas had been pushed back from 2026 to 2027.
Today's 5.21% fall adds to a pattern that has been relentless since February 28. South Korea's net oil imports represent 2.7% of its GDP — making it one of the most structurally vulnerable economies in the world to oil price shocks, according to Nomura. With Brent crude near $112 per barrel, Korean manufacturing companies across every sector face soaring input costs, compressed margins, and weakening export competitiveness. Daniel Yoo, global market strategist at Yuanta Securities, has described the KOSPI's recent drop as "partly driven by profit-taking after a strong runup amidst a risk-off environment" combined with "growing concern that AI datacenter adoption pace might slow due to significantly higher energy costs."
Japan's Nikkei 225: 50,919 and Falling
Japan's Nikkei 225 is suffering its own severe correction today, trading at 50,919.43 — down 2,453.10 points, or 4.59%, from its previous close of 53,372.53. This is a significant single-day loss for an index that was trading above 59,000 as recently as late 2025. The Nikkei's day range of 50,945.45 to 52,479.81 shows the index opened near its intraday high and has been falling steadily throughout the morning session. The previous close of 53,372.53 against today's open of 52,468.72 already signalled a gap-down open — and the selling has continued from there.
Japan, like South Korea, is a massive oil importer with a manufacturing-heavy economy that is extremely sensitive to energy price shocks. Export-oriented companies — the backbone of the Nikkei — face a dual squeeze: higher energy costs compressing margins, and uncertainty about global demand as recession fears spread across the US and European markets. Consumer cyclicals have been particularly hard hit in today's session. The broader Topix index has also fallen in parallel with the Nikkei.
Japan's situation is further complicated by currency dynamics. A prolonged oil shock typically weakens the yen against the dollar as Japan's current account deteriorates — but a weaker yen also makes Japan's exports cheaper, partially offsetting the pain for exporters. The net effect has been significant volatility in both equity and currency markets, with investors positioning cautiously ahead of further geopolitical developments.
What Triggered Today's Specific Selloff?
While the Iran war has been the background driver of Asian market weakness for three weeks, today's specific intensification appears linked to weekend developments. Trump's 48-hour ultimatum to Iran to reopen the Strait of Hormuz — which he threatened to enforce by bombing Iranian power plants — has not produced a ceasefire. Iran's new Supreme Leader publicly stated that the Strait should remain shut. With no diplomatic off-ramp visible and oil prices stubbornly high, investors who had been hoping for a quick resolution are now pricing in a prolonged conflict scenario. The CNBC article from Friday, March 20 noted that "Asia-Pacific markets fell as oil prices soared on renewed fears that a prolonged conflict in the Middle East could further crimp energy supplies, stoking fears of a global economic downturn." Today's session appears to be a continuation and intensification of that Friday selloff.
Additionally, overnight US market weakness is feeding into Asia. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell nearly 740 points last week to settle below 47,000 for the first time in 2026. The S&P 500 shed 1.5% and the Nasdaq lost 1.8% in recent sessions. When Wall Street falls, Asian markets typically follow — the connection between US and Asian equities has strengthened significantly in the era of globalised institutional investment flows.
How Long Could This Last?
Analyst consensus is divided, but the majority view suggests continued volatility until one of three things happens: a ceasefire or meaningful de-escalation in the Iran conflict, a significant drop in oil prices from current levels, or evidence that the Federal Reserve will support markets through rate cuts. None of these catalysts appears imminent. The Kalshi prediction market has raised recession probability bets for the US to their highest levels of 2026. Goldman Sachs is projecting elevated oil prices through 2027 in a base case of prolonged conflict. For Asian equity markets — particularly South Korea and Japan — this macro backdrop is deeply unfavourable.
Key levels to watch on the KOSPI: the 5,093 floor from the circuit-breaker day of March 3 represents the most recent major support. A break below that level would be psychologically devastating and could trigger further institutional selling. On the Nikkei, the 50,000 level — which the index has briefly traded below intraday today at 50,919 — is a critical round-number support. From $70 to $112: How the Iran War's Oil Shock Is Destroying Stock Markets From Seoul to Mumbai — And When It Might Stop
By HedwigPost Markets Desk  ·  March 23, 2026  ·  Markets Analysis  ·  11 min read
CATEGORY: Stock Market | TAGS: Brent crude $112, oil price stock market, Iran war oil shock, global market crash, India inflation, FII outflows, RBI rate cut

  THE ROOT CAUSE: Every major market decline you are seeing today — KOSPI -5.21%, Nikkei -4.59%, GIFT Nifty -1.26%, Sensex -13% YTD — traces back to a single variable: Brent crude oil at approximately $112 per barrel. Understanding the transmission mechanism from oil to equities is the key to understanding this entire market environment.
The Numbers That Started Everything
On February 27, 2026 — the last trading day before the United States and Israel launched strikes on Iran — Brent crude was trading at approximately $70 per barrel. It was a comfortable, unremarkable level. Inflation globally was cooling. Central banks were considering rate cuts. Equity markets across the world were near all-time highs.
Then February 28 happened. The strikes on Iran triggered an immediate and sustained oil price surge that represents one of the largest percentage moves in crude oil in decades. Brent crude surged from $70 to above $80 within hours of the strikes being announced. By early March, as Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz and attacked Gulf energy infrastructure, WTI jumped 20% in a single week and Brent rose 17%. By March 20, Brent had touched $112.19 per barrel — its highest closing level since July 2022 during the post-COVID energy crisis. The move from $70 to $112 represents a 60% increase in the price of the world's most important commodity in less than four weeks.

The Transmission Mechanism: How $112 Oil Crashes Every Stock Market
Step 1: Inflation Expectations Jump Immediately
Oil is not just fuel — it is an input cost in the production of almost everything. When oil rises 60% in four weeks, companies that use energy, manufacture goods, run logistics fleets, or transport products all face higher costs simultaneously. Economists calculate that a sustained $10 increase in oil prices historically adds 0.3–0.4 percentage points to US CPI inflation. A $42 increase — from $70 to $112 — could, if sustained, add over 1.5 percentage points to US inflation. In a world where central banks are already nervous about inflation not returning to their 2% targets fast enough, this is devastating. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment survey, taken after February 28, showed a sharp deterioration that completely erased all the improvements recorded before the war.
Step 2: Central Banks Cannot Cut Rates
Rate cuts are the single most powerful tailwind for equity markets — they reduce borrowing costs, improve corporate earnings forecasts, and drive investors out of bonds and into stocks. Before February 28, markets were pricing in two to three Federal Reserve rate cuts in 2026. Within weeks of the war, those expectations were entirely cancelled, according to CME Group data. The Fed has since kept rates unchanged with a hawkish tone, with Governor Christopher Waller explicitly flagging inflation concerns linked to the oil shock. Some desks are now pricing a small probability of a rate hike. For equity markets, this is the worst possible central bank backdrop.
Step 3: Corporate Earnings Forecasts Are Slashed
Higher input costs for energy-intensive industries flow directly into lower profit margins. Airlines, shipping companies, automobile manufacturers, chemical companies, and heavy industry all face significant earnings downgrades when oil stays above $100 for extended periods. For Korean and Japanese companies — where energy-intensive manufacturing and exports are core to their business models — the margin compression is particularly severe. This is why Samsung, SK Hynix, Sony, Toyota and other Asian industrial giants are seeing the sharpest share price declines: analysts are downgrading their earnings forecasts in real time.
Step 4: FII Flows Reverse — Emerging Markets Bear the Brunt
When global risk sentiment deteriorates — driven by inflation fears, central bank hawkishness, and geopolitical uncertainty — foreign institutional investors systematically reduce their exposure to emerging market equities. India, South Korea, and other Asian markets see significant FII outflows as global funds rotate into US dollars, US Treasury bonds, and other safe-haven assets. South Korea saw foreign investors sell a record 7.12 trillion won ($5 billion) of Korean equities in a single session. India has seen sustained FII selling throughout March. This relentless selling pressure amplifies every downward move in these indices.
Step 5: Currency Weakness Compounds the Problem
As FIIs sell Asian equities, they also sell Asian currencies to repatriate proceeds. The Indian rupee has crossed ₹93 per dollar — a record low — and other Asian currencies have similarly weakened against the USD. Currency weakness makes imports more expensive (worsening inflation), increases the cost of foreign-currency debt servicing, and further erodes the purchasing power of domestic consumers. For India specifically, where crude oil is the largest single import item, a weaker rupee on top of higher oil prices creates a double whammy for the current account deficit.
Which Asian Economy Is Most Vulnerable?
South Korea tops the vulnerability ranking by every measure analysts use. Net oil imports of 2.7% of GDP (Nomura), a stock market concentrated 50% in two semiconductor companies, and an export-heavy economy make it the perfect target for an oil shock. Japan is second — large oil imports, manufacturing-heavy, and currently facing a complex monetary policy environment as the Bank of Japan navigates its historic exit from ultra-loose monetary policy. India is third but has meaningful buffers: a large domestic economy that reduces export dependence, strong forex reserves, growing DII participation in domestic markets, and a central bank with more rate-cutting room than the Fed.
When Does the Oil Shock End? Key Scenarios to Watch
Scenario 1 — Quick ceasefire (2–4 weeks): If the Trump ultimatum produces a negotiated Strait of Hormuz reopening within weeks, oil prices could fall sharply back toward $80–90. Rate cut expectations would partially recover. Markets would bounce significantly. This is the "best case" scenario but appears low probability based on current positions of both Iran and the US-Israel coalition.
Scenario 2 — Prolonged war, manageable (3–6 months): Oil stays in the $90–110 range. US enters a mild economic slowdown but avoids recession. Markets trade in a volatile, directionless range. Indian Nifty finds support around 22,000–22,500. This is Goldman Sachs' base case for the next six months.
Scenario 3 — Extended war with escalation (6+ months): Oil above $120 for a sustained period. US recession materialises in Q4 2026. Global equity markets enter a deep bear phase. Indian Nifty could test its 52-week low of 21,828 or break below it. This is the tail risk scenario that markets are beginning to price with increasing probability.

The duration and intensity of the Iran conflict will determine whether the lows are already in or whether there is further pain ahead.
